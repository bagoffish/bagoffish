Goodnight

She washed me as if the day had been invented for that purpose.

Morning was the pale stripe that climbed the far wall and stopped at the water stain. Afternoon was the heat that collected in the sheets and made the cotton stick where I could no longer kick it off. Evening was the lamp she never quite turned off, only dimmed, as if darkness itself might steal me if she looked away.

Her hands were warm. Soft cloth. Water that had been tested against her wrist first. She worked in circles over my mouth, my throat, the sour film in the folds of my elbows, the waste a body produces when it has been left in one position too long. She never made a face. Even when the muscles jumped without my permission, she only waited, then continued, as if disgust were a language she had never learned.

I could not move enough to help her. I could not move enough to stop her. When my head slipped, she was already there, palm under the nape, catching me the way a person catches a cup that has been sliding toward the edge all afternoon.

She was not my mother.

Mothers have the particular smell of a house you are allowed to leave. She had none of that. She was not a sister. Not a friend doing a favor that would someday be mentioned in a kitchen. The nurse who came with a badge and gloves came only twice a week, asked about deliveries, turned me on a clock, and wrote in a chart. This woman was there when the nurse was not. She had pale hair that fell forward when she leaned. A dress the color of old roses. A way of humming without words.

"Who are you?" I asked once, when the words lined up long enough to be used.

She smiled the way people smile at a question that has arrived too late in the day.

"I'm here," she said. "That's enough for today."

It was not enough. It was the only thing I had.

The rented bed stood in the living room because the hallway to the bedroom was too narrow for the chair. A hospital van had brought me home. Two men and a nurse who was not her had lifted me through my own doorway, opened the windows, shown me a call button I could just reach with the arm that still listened, and left. No one else lived here. The refrigerator had gone sour before I returned. There was a second spoon in a drawer I did not remember placing there.

I told myself she had come with the bed. People appear in the aftermath of a fall. Social workers. Neighbors who suddenly remember your name. A woman who stays and wrings a cloth is only a more intimate version of the same mercy.

I wanted that to be true.

She did not eat when I could see her. She did not sleep in the chair so much as wait in it. She had no keys in the dish by the door. When I asked her to leave a toothbrush, she said she had one and never produced it. At night I heard cups in the kitchen and then, when the nurse came in the morning, there was only one set of footprints in the dust.

"Do you have someone staying overnight?" the nurse asked.

"No."

The nurse looked at the kitchen doorway anyway, the way a person looks at a room that feels occupied.

I did not correct the feeling. I had lived too long in rooms that felt the opposite.

Before the bed there had been ordinary days, and then days that only resembled them.

I am not going to give you a map of the fall. Concrete. A sound I felt more than heard. A stranger's palms on either side of my head and a voice saying "don't move", which was already a joke by the time it arrived. An ambulance that smelled like rubber and rain. Questions: name, age, who to call.

"No one," I said.

They did not take me anywhere except a trauma bay. They talked over me about swelling and breath. I kept the breath. I did not keep the rest. Weeks of straps and other people's optimism. A social worker with a gentle voice asked whether there was a partner who should be informed.

"She left," I said.

The social worker wrote it down as if it were an address.

I let her.

The woman at the bed massaged the stiffness from my shoulders as if she had a right to know every place I hurt. When my eyes burned and water ran sideways into my hair, she said it was all right, she was doing this for me.

"Don't," I told her. "Don't make it kind."

She did not stop.

There were thoughts that did not feel authored by me. They arrived already finished. A sentence in the hallway. My name spoken from the kitchen when the kitchen was empty. The nurse called it poor insight later, after I asked why the overnight guest policy did not apply to the woman in the dress. Hallucinations, the chart said. Person-specific. History of collapse. Recent spinal injury. They liked words that made a mess look filed.

I told the psychiatrist I wanted to get better.

That was true.

I also wanted the cloth to keep moving.

Those two wants shared a body that could not sit up without help.

The first crack was small.

She was wiping my mouth when the nurse's key turned in the lock, early. The woman at the bed did not startle the way a living person startles. She only stepped back into the kitchen doorway and became, for a moment, a coat on a hook, a trick of the lamp. The nurse saw me. Only me. She spoke to me as if the air behind her were ordinary air.

After the nurse left, the woman came back already holding the cloth, as if no time had passed.

"You didn't say hello to her," I said.

"She isn't here for me."

I laughed once, dry. Then I stopped laughing, because the second spoon was still in the drawer, and the soap I had bought—her soap, the one from the shop near the station—was still sealed on my sink, and I had not been able to open it since I came home.

Guilt does not always introduce itself. Sometimes it just stocks your bathroom.

I began to remember in the wrong order.

An argument that had lived in someone else's walls. A silence she used when she had already decided something. The kettle clicking off. A scooter under a window that was not this window. A phone lighting up and going dark. My legs hurting from sitting on a floor, which is a pain I would now pay for.

I tried to see her face the way it had been when it could still change its mind. I could not. The face over the bed was the face I could bear. The other one stayed attached to a weight I was not ready to lift with the one arm that worked.

"Who are you?" I asked again.

She wrung the cloth. Water ticked into the basin.

"You know," she said. "You only ask when you want me to be someone else."

They put her on the television on a Thursday.

Not the woman in the dress, I told myself. A small photograph in the corner of a regional segment. Employee not seen at work. Family unable to contact. Building manager prepared to enter if the rent lapsed. I watched with my head strapped to the pillow. The woman beside the bed watched with me, head tilted, as if inspecting a bad likeness.

"They got the workplace wrong," she said.

"I can call."

"And say what?"

The arm jumped and spilled nothing.

She lifted my head so I would not choke, and for one long second the two pictures refused to separate: a face the city was still willing to call missing, and hands on my throat that had no pulse I could feel except the one they borrowed from me.

I did not kill her in this apartment.

You need that, or the van, the nurses, the men who carried me through my own door, become impossible. There was another room. A keypad. A neighbor who worked nights. A family in another city. Automatic rent. A habit she had of answering late, which is a habit the world mistakes for life until it runs out.

I am not going to rebuild the night for you. Vacancy does not always look like rage. Sometimes it looks like a person wanting to know whether the world will change shape if something irreversible is done inside it. The world did not change shape. I locked a door that was not mine. I took a phone because a ringing phone is how quiet rooms get opened. I sent the kind of messages a tired woman sends when she wants to be left alone. Then I came back here, to the address that would later receive a hospital van, and I waited for a city large enough to be late.

The voices started before the concrete.

Not screaming. Asking why the rice had not been cooked. Why the bathroom light was still on. Why I had not turned her. I bought the soap. I could not open it. I told a clinic doctor I could not sleep. He wrote anxiety on a pad. I went to work until the stairwell started answering me. I did not go back to her building to finish anything. I finished myself in public enough to be found by strangers, not by a landlord. They cut the clothes off in the ambulance. They never asked for a second set of keys.

That is how a man comes home without a body in the other room being carried in behind him.

The body stays where the life was.

The man is delivered to where the guilt lives.

There is a smell in the stairwell now that I tell myself is the drain.

The nurse put on a second pair of gloves last visit. She asked about more hours. She looked at the kitchen doorway as if the doorway had almost spoken. After she left, the woman at the bed sat on the edge of the mattress, dress the color of something that had once been a flower, and smoothed the sheet over legs that could not feel the gesture.

"When they open my door," she said, "you should not be surprised. You left the heat off. You left the light on. You always forget the light."

"Don't."

"I'm not angry about the light."

She said it softly.

I asked her to stay in this room. I asked it the way you ask when you already know the answer is split: one part of her here, leaning, washing, humming; the rest of her still in a one-room walk-up running out of ordinary reasons not to open.
