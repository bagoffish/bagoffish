Talking about love

I think one of the hardest things to accept is that some people leave without becoming villains.

That sounds obvious until it happens to you.

When someone hurts us, there is a strange comfort in being able to point at a betrayal and say *that* is why it ended. They lied. They cheated. They stopped caring. They became cruel. Those stories are painful, but at least they are understandable. They let grief organize itself into something with a cause.

But many people do not leave because they stopped loving you in some dramatic way.

Sometimes they just keep becoming themselves... and eventually that person is no longer someone who can stay.

That is much harder to grieve.

Because there is no enemy.

No final mistake.

Just time doing what time always does: changing people quietly until the relationship that once felt inevitable starts feeling like something both of you are accidentally preserving out of habit.

And habits can look a lot like love from close enough.

I think that is why some breakups haunt people for years. Not because they were especially catastrophic, but because they were ordinary. There was no scene. No final argument important enough to replay. Maybe one day they simply took longer to answer. Then the conversations became practical instead of intimate. Then you noticed you were the only one asking questions. Then one day you realized you had been mourning the relationship for months while still technically inside it.

That kind of ending can make you doubt your own memory.

You start revisiting everything, trying to find the exact point where the emotional gravity changed.

Was it that night you were too tired to respond properly?

That argument that seemed small at the time?

The day they met someone new and you did not realize what it would mean?

Or was the ending already inside the beginning somehow... just too quiet to hear?

Soren Kierkegaard once wrote that life can only be understood backward, but it must be lived forward.

I think that may be why heartbreak feels so cruel.

Because once a relationship ends, the past becomes unbearable to look at. Every memory acquires information you did not have when you were living it. The first time they hesitated before saying "I love you" back. The last time they looked distracted while you were talking. The small silences you ignored because you believed love was stronger than temporary distance.

You do not just remember what happened.

You reinterpret it.

And sometimes that reinterpretation hurts more than the loss itself.

Because it makes you wonder if you were loving a future that had already quietly disappeared.

But I also think people misunderstand what it means when someone leaves.

It is easy to believe their absence means the connection was false. That if they can move on, laugh with someone else, then what you had must have been less real than it felt.

That is almost never true.

People can leave something real.

That is what makes leaving tragic.

If relationships only ended when they were fake, heartbreak would not feel the way it does. The reason it destroys people is precisely because something meaningful existed, and still was not enough to stop change. Love does not freeze anyone in place. It does not suspend growth. Sometimes two people are sincere and still become incompatible simply because they continued becoming themselves.

That is not a failure.

It just does not feel survivable when you are inside it.

Especially when they meet someone new.

That part can feel almost metaphysical in its cruelty.

Because the person you loved does not just leave your life — they begin constructing a new world that does not require you. New routines. New jokes. New tenderness. Someone else learns the things you once knew first. Someone else gets the version of them you spent years helping shape. And there is something deeply painful about realizing the person who felt irreplaceable to you can still become familiar to someone else.

It makes love feel frighteningly transferable.

Like all the things you thought were sacred might simply happen again... just without you.

But maybe that is not the right way to see it.

Because no relationship is repeatable, even when the surface looks similar.

The person they become with someone else is not the person they were with you.

And the person you were with them is gone too.

That is the part people rarely talk about. When someone leaves, you do not just lose them. You lose access to a specific version of yourself that only existed in relation to them. The way you laughed. The things you noticed. The future you imagined while lying awake next to them. That self does not entirely survive the breakup. Some of it stays in the old apartment, in the messages you deleted, in the restaurant you no longer visit because it would feel too strange to sit there alone.

That is why moving on can feel like betrayal.

Not because you still love them necessarily, but because healing requires allowing parts of that old self to die. And there is grief in that too. People talk about missing another person, but sometimes what you miss most is the person you were when they still chose you.

Albert Camus wrote about absurdity as the confrontation between our need for meaning and the silence of the world.

Relationships teach a version of that.

You want there to be a reason someone stopped loving you. A lesson. A flaw to correct. Some explanation that makes pain proportional. But often there is only silence. People change. They choose differently. They feel something and then stop feeling it. Not because the universe is punishing you, but because another consciousness remained free in ways you could never control.

That is terrifying.

And also... strangely hopeful.

Because the same unpredictability that allowed one person to leave is what allows someone new to arrive.

That is easy to forget when your heart is still organized around someone who no longer lives there. But eventually, often when you are not looking for it, another person enters your life in a way that feels almost impossible to notice at first. A conversation that lasts longer than expected. A message you reread for no reason. The first time someone makes you laugh and you realize you had forgotten your own laugh sounded like that.

It never feels like replacing the person who left.

It feels more like discovering that grief did not close you. It only changed the shape of the door.

That matters.

Because after heartbreak, many people secretly believe they have already experienced the most important love of their life and everything else will just be imitation. But that is usually grief speaking. The mind treats what was lost as singular because loss magnifies meaning. It turns ordinary moments into relics.

New love does not erase that.

It just reveals something quieter: that the heart is not loyal to one story forever.

It can carry multiple ruins and still build again.

That does not mean forgetting.

Some people stay with you forever in ways that have nothing to do with whether you still speak. They remain in your habits. In the way you hesitate before opening certain apps. In the specific silence that falls on some winter evenings. In how you still instinctively reach for your phone when something funny happens, even years later, before remembering there is no one to send it to.

But that is not the same as being trapped.

It just means you were changed.

And being changed by love is not something to be ashamed of.

I think that may be the entire tragedy and beauty of human relationships.

People leave.

People return.

People become strangers.

Strangers become the person you tell everything to at 2 a.m.

Someone you once thought you could not survive without becomes a gentle ache you only notice when certain weather arrives.

And someone you have not met yet may one day walk into your life so naturally that it will scare you how possible happiness still was all along.

That does not invalidate the people who left.

It honors them, in a strange way.

Because every person who loved you, even briefly, altered the person who will be loved next.

Nothing is wasted.

Not the heartbreak.

Not the waiting.

Not the nights you thought no one would ever understand you again.

All of it remains somewhere inside the next version of you... the one who has not met them yet, but will.

And maybe that is enough reason to keep going.

Not because everything happens for a reason.

But because sometimes the reason only becomes visible after you survive long enough to meet the person who makes your old grief look like a road rather than a destination.

And if you survive long enough to become someone new, do not forget this: you were never only important to the people who stayed. You mattered even in the lives that could not keep you.
