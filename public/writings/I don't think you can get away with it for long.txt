I don't think you can get away with it for long. Just bury him here, vertically, 4 meters deep.

I killed a man in winter, though I cannot say with certainty which winter it was.

That is the first difficulty. Not that I killed him—of that I am as certain as a man can be of anything that happened long ago—but that the season has swallowed the year whole. Winter repeats itself too faithfully. Every snowfall resembles the last. Every bare tree holds the same expression of accusation. Time, once it has been touched by guilt, ceases to proceed in a straight line; it decays. It softens at the edges. It caves beneath recollection like old floorboards under the weight of someone walking above you in the dark.

Still, I remember the cold.

Not the abstract cold one speaks of indoors, wrapped in blankets, with the ondol heating beneath the floor and a kettle murmuring on the stove. I mean the kind that enters through the fingernails first. The kind that makes the joints of the hand ache before the skin has even begun to sting. The air that evening was so sharp it seemed to have corners. Each breath cut the throat on its way down. The snow had fallen earlier in the day and then stopped, leaving the wooded hillside unnaturally still, as if every sound had been packed under the white crust and buried there. My boots sank with a muffled crunch that sounded too loud to me, though in truth there was no one near enough to hear.

The road was not much of a road, only a narrow strip of frozen dirt that climbed through the trees above the old villages outside Incheon, where the city thinned into farmland and the sea wind came inland through the bare branches. The sort of road that appears on no map unless one has lived nearby for generations. I had walked it many times before, though never with a knife in my coat pocket.

It was an ordinary knife. That detail matters. I had purchased it two months earlier from a hardware shop near the old bus terminal, whose owner wore spectacles so thick they made his eyes appear detached from his face. It had a wooden handle, slightly black, and a blade just under 12 centimeters. I remember the price—exactly 12,000 won—because I had thought at the time that it was too much for such a plain thing. There was a small notch near the hilt where the metal had not been polished properly. My thumb found that imperfection often. It was there beneath my glove as I walked that evening, rubbing the same notch over and over as if confirming that the knife was real and not merely an intention.

I had not gone out with a plan. Or so I told myself then.

There had been no quarrel. No debt. No insult that demanded satisfaction. I had spent the afternoon in my rented room, staring at the wallpaper peeling near the stove, unable to read, unable to sleep, unable even to think in a coherent sequence. A pressure had been building in me for weeks, perhaps months—a sensation not unlike standing at the top of a staircase and feeling, with dreadful fascination, how easy it would be to let oneself fall. There are impulses that arrive not from hatred but from vacancy. One wants to see whether the world will change shape if something irreversible is done to it.

So I walked.

Dusk came early. It always does in winter. The light thinned between the trees until everything seemed submerged, as if the entire hillside had sunk to the bottom of a frozen sea. It was then I saw him.

He stood at the side of the road, near a cedar whose branches had long since died and hardened into black claws against the sky. He was not doing anything remarkable. That is what horrifies me now. He was simply standing there, reading a letter. His hat was pushed slightly back from his forehead, and he held the page close to his face because the light was failing. Once he lifted his gloved hand and brushed snow from the paper. Such a small gesture. So human. So unprepared for what was already approaching him from behind.

I stopped when I first saw him.

The distance between us could not have been more than twenty paces. Had he turned, he would have seen me plainly. But he did not. His entire attention belonged to the letter in his hands. I remember the exact sound of the paper rustling in the sea wind. I remember the way one of his boots was half-buried in a drift near the roadside. I remember thinking—not in words, but with a kind of blank certainty—that if I kept walking, then something would happen that could never be undone.

I did keep walking.

My heartbeat did not quicken. That surprises me even now. One imagines murder as a frenzy, a storm of blood and panic. But there was only an extraordinary stillness, as though I had stepped out of myself and was watching from somewhere behind my own eyes. My hand went into my coat pocket. The knife came free. I do not remember deciding to draw it. The body has its own convictions, and they are often more decisive than the mind.

He heard me when I was perhaps two steps behind him.

He began to turn. Not fully—just enough that his shoulder shifted, enough that I saw the corner of his eye. My left hand seized the collar of his coat and pulled backward. The fabric was rough wool, damp with melted snow. My fingers slipped once before finding purchase. He gasped—not a scream, not even a word, just a short involuntary intake of breath, the kind a person makes when cold seawater is spilled down their back.

Then my right hand moved.

The knife entered beneath his ribs.

That is where I had imagined it would go, though I did not know until that instant that I had imagined anything at all. There was resistance at first: the heavy cloth of his coat, then the thickness of the shirt beneath. After that, nothing. The blade passed through him with an ease so obscene it offended me. Flesh should resist. Bone should intervene. But it was as though the body had already opened itself to receive the steel.

He dropped the letter.

I remember seeing it fall face-up into the snow, the black lines of writing stark against the white. It landed without sound.

He made a noise then, a wet choking noise, and twisted with a strength I had not expected. We slipped together. My boots lost purchase. We both went down into the snow, and the knife tore sideways as he fell. Warmth burst over my hand. For a moment I thought I had cut myself, but it was his blood, soaking instantly through my glove, startling in its heat. Steam rose where it struck the snow.

And then he looked at me.

No hatred. No rage. Not even fear.

Only confusion.

A terrible, intimate bewilderment, as though he were trying to place me. As though my face belonged somewhere in his childhood or in a dream he had once forgotten and was only now remembering. His lips moved. He said something, but the blood in his throat turned the words into a bubbling whisper. I leaned closer without meaning to, desperate to hear.

I think he said my name.

But that cannot be true, because I had never seen him before.

He died less than a minute later.

The body did not thrash dramatically as in cheap novels. It shuddered. That is the word. A series of diminishing shudders, each weaker than the last, until even those ceased. His eyes remained open. Snow gathered on his lashes. One flake landed on the dark pupil and did not melt.

I stood over him for some time. I do not know how long. The hillside had become completely silent. Even the wind from the harbor seemed to have withdrawn, unwilling to participate. My breath smoked above us in pale clouds. My right hand still held the knife. I remember noticing that my wrist hurt from gripping it too tightly.

Then practicality asserted itself.

I dragged him off the road by his ankles. He was heavier than I expected, and his coat kept catching on the frozen roots beneath the snow. His head struck stones as we moved. Each impact produced a dull sound I can still hear if I wake in the night. Once his arm flopped upward and the hand brushed my boot. I recoiled as though he had grabbed me.

The cedar stood perhaps twenty yards into the trees, overlooking the distant gray sea. It was dead, split by lightning long ago, and one side of its trunk had rotted away, exposing thick roots that twisted from the ground like the ribs of some buried animal. I chose it because the earth there looked softer. It was not.

The frost had hardened the soil to the consistency of brick. I had no shovel. I used a broken branch first, then the knife itself, then my bare hands when the branch snapped. The cold burned so intensely it ceased to feel cold and became merely pain. Dirt packed under my nails. The skin across my knuckles split. My breath came raggedly. Several times I stopped and listened because I thought I heard footsteps on the road above, but it was only the blood pounding in my ears.

The hole was too shallow.

I knew it even as I lowered him into it. His knees would not straighten fully. One arm bent awkwardly beneath his back. I almost pulled him out to dig deeper, but the sight of his face tilted toward me from the grave—those eyes still open, snow melting into them—caused something in my chest to seize. I shoved soil over him as quickly as I could, scarcely caring whether he was covered properly.

At one point the hand emerged again, fingers protruding through the loose earth as if reaching upward.

I struck it with the branch until it disappeared.

When it was done, I stood over the patch of disturbed ground, panting, covered in mud and blood, and began to laugh.

Not because it was funny. Because the silence had become unbearable. Because if I had not laughed, I think I would have screamed so loudly the entire hillside above Incheon would have answered.

I moved away the following spring.

No one came for me.

That was the second difficulty: the realization that ordinary life has no way of noticing what has been done to it unless someone speaks.
