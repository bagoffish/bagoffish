Untitled

I have been carrying my mental illness on my back lately, though I cannot say with certainty when it first settled there. It clings like damp wool in winter, shifting with every step, sometimes light enough to ignore and sometimes so heavy that each breath feels borrowed. The voices are part of it now. Clear sometimes, murmuring others, always present. They comment on what I do, argue about what I should do next, and lately one of them has grown louder, more insistent. I came to the hospital with a sincere desire to fix my personal flaws. That much is true. I wanted the fractures in my thinking mended, the paranoia quieted, the days returned to something ordinary. I believed the doctors could help. I still want to believe it, even after everything.

I only realized the truth after reviewing the medical records at the pharmacy. I had gone to collect the latest refill. While the pharmacist turned away, I leaned over the counter and saw the screen. My name. The dates. The notes from multiple doctors, all echoing the same verdict: schizophrenia, paranoid type, poor insight, command hallucinations, risk of harm. They had written me down as a psychiatric patient before I had finished explaining myself. It felt like a conspiracy, not the dramatic kind with secret meetings, but the quiet, institutional kind where everyone agrees on the label and stops listening to the man wearing it. The other doctors said the same things. Even when I did not want to hear it, they told me anyway. It was so frustrating.

When I get examined, they show no real interest. They sit behind their desks, glance at the file, and ask questions that feel scripted. I describe the voices that argue in my head, the way certain thoughts arrive fully formed and refuse to leave, the paranoia that makes every stranger on the street seem to know something about me. They laugh. Not loudly, but in that small, professional way that dismisses without seeming to dismiss. They brush it off roughly. "Common at this stage," one said once, as if my entire life had become a stage they were watching from above. I came to the hospital because I wanted to fix this. I told them about the flaws I could feel growing inside me. The disorganization, the suspicion, the exhaustion of fighting thoughts that were not entirely mine. Instead, they keep prescribing strange medications that turn me into a lunatic.

The side effects are relentless. I cannot focus. Words slide off the page when I try to read. Conversations fragment in the middle. My body feels wired and heavy at the same time, legs restless, mind fogged, a constant low hum behind my eyes that makes ordinary tasks feel impossible. My daily life has become impossible. It is clearly the medication's fault. Fuck. I take them anyway because the alternative is the voices growing louder, the world tilting further. But the pills do not silence anything; they warp it into something slower and more grotesque.

And then there is the other doctor. Not the main one I see for appointments, but another whose voice slips in during the quiet moments. He whispers. He tells me the prescriptions are poison, that they are turning me into the patient they want me to be, that the only way to stop the conspiracy is to stop him. I want to kill him. The urge keeps rising from deep inside, raw and urgent, like something clawing its way out. Every dawn I cannot hold back the impulse and end up grabbing a knife and standing in front of the house. The blade feels real in my hand. The cold air sharpens everything. I stand there until the sun begins to rise, fighting the command, shaking with effort. I feel like I need to clearly inform the doctor of my condition. I even planned to kill the doctor. Even if my friends report me, I do not care. I am destined to become a criminal anyway. But I really want to get better. I wish the voices in my head would just disappear.

I decided to tell the doctor everything so he could give me one last chance. I sat across from him in the small, sterile room. The fluorescent light buzzed overhead. I laid it all out: the records I had seen, the side effects that were destroying my days, the whispering voice, the knife at dawn, the plan I had made. I told him I was scared of what I might do if nothing changed. I told him I still wanted help.

The doctor tried to lock me up in the psychiatric ward. Is that really going to go according to your plan? At least the doctor seemed scared. His face changed when I described the urge and the plan. His eyes widened slightly, the professional mask cracking. He changed a lot of my prescriptions. Different pills, different doses, something stronger or gentler, I could not tell. If he realizes I could kill him, he will listen to my story carefully now, right? I wish the doctor would not give up on me. Please. I wish the doctor would not abandon me. Please.

The new medications brought their own distortions. The voices did not leave; they multiplied, layering over one another until conversations inside my head felt like crowded rooms. I would catch myself answering them aloud on the street, then look around to see strangers staring. Thoughts arrived in fragments, jumping from one fear to another without bridges. I saw patterns in everything. The way people walked, the timing of traffic lights, the arrangement of papers on the doctor's desk. All of it evidence of the conspiracy to turn me permanently into this version of myself. The patient. The case file. The one who could no longer be trusted with his own life.

Yet I kept returning for appointments. I kept hoping. The main doctor now watched me more closely. His notes, I imagined, grew longer. When the whispering voice grew especially loud, urging me to act before it was too late, I would grip the arms of the chair and force myself to speak instead. "The voices are worse," I would say. "The urge is stronger." He would nod, adjust the dose again, and suggest the ward once more. Each time he looked a little more tired, a little more wary. I could see my own future in his eyes: locked away, medicated into stillness, labeled forever.

Some nights the illness feels almost tender. The voices soften and tell me I am right to fight. Other nights they scream that I am already lost, that the doctors have won and the only freedom left is to make the whispering stop permanently. I stand at the window with the knife in my hand and watch the street below, imagining how easy it would be to cross the distance between what I am and what they say I have become. Then the desire to get better surges again, raw and desperate, and I put the knife away. I want the real me back. The one who could focus, who did not suspect every kindness, who did not hear judgments in every silence.

I still go to the hospital. I still sit across from the doctor and search his face for any sign that he sees the person and not just the diagnosis. Sometimes, in the middle of a sentence, the whispering starts again, and I lose the thread of what I was saying. The doctor waits. He looks scared, but he also looks determined. He changes the prescriptions once more. He listens or at least pretends to. I leave each time with a fragile hope that this time the new tablets will quiet the storm, that this time he will not give up.

The illness rides my back heavier than ever, but I keep walking. I keep wanting to get better. The voices rise and fall. The knife waits in the drawer. The doctor's face lingers in my mind, tired, wary, maybe even a little kind. I wish he would not abandon me. Please. I wish none of them would. I came here to fix this. I am still here, trying.

But the doctor has not abandoned me by choice. That last dawn, when the voice grew too loud to argue with, I did not put the knife away. I walked to the hospital while the sky was still gray. I sat across from him in the same sterile room and told him everything, exactly as I have told it here. His eyes widened when I described the plan, when I showed him the blade I had carried inside my coat. He reached for the alarm button beneath his desk, the one that would have locked the doors, summoned orderlies, ended everything. That was when I crossed the room.

The fluorescent light still buzzes. The blood on the floor has long since dried into the pattern the voices once showed me in the papers on his desk. I took the new prescription he had begun to write in those final seconds; the handwriting is shaky, almost illegible, but I fill it anyway. Sometimes I return to the empty office and sit in the chair across from where he used to be. I tell him how the voices have multiplied. I ask him to adjust the dose. He never answers, yet I hear his voice anyway. Tired, wary, still trying to help the man who killed him.

I keep the knife in the drawer. I still want to get better. The real me is somewhere beneath all this, I am certain of it. I just must keep walking until the illness grows light enough to set down. The doctor cannot abandon me now. He is with me always.
